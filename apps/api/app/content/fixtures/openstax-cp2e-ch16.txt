# 16.1 Hooke's Law: Stress and Strain Revisited
================================================================================

Newton's first law implies that an object oscillating back and forth experiences
forces. Without force, the object would move in a straight line at constant
speed rather than oscillate. When a plastic ruler is plucked to the side,
deformation creates a restoring force in the opposite direction. Once released,
this restoring force moves the ruler back toward its stable equilibrium
position, where net force equals zero. However, the ruler gains momentum and
continues moving, producing opposite deformation. This process repeats until
dissipative forces dampen the motion, gradually reducing oscillation until the
ruler comes to rest.

The simplest oscillations occur when the restoring force is directly
proportional to displacement. This relationship between force and displacement
is Hooke's law:

    F = −k x

Here, F is the restoring force, x is displacement from equilibrium or
deformation, and k is a constant related to deformation difficulty. The minus
sign indicates the restoring force opposes displacement direction.

The force constant k relates to system rigidity — larger force constants
indicate greater restoring forces and stiffer systems. Units of k are newtons
per meter (N/m). k is directly related to Young's modulus when stretching a
string.

## Energy in Hooke's Law of Deformation

To produce deformation, work must be done. If no work converts to thermal,
sound, or kinetic energy, all work is initially stored as potential energy.
The potential energy stored in a spring is:

    PE_el = ½ k x²

where PE_el is elastic potential energy in any deformed system obeying Hooke's
law, with displacement x from equilibrium and force constant k.

Work performed by applied force F_app equals the area under a force-vs-
deformation graph. Since force increases linearly from 0 to k x, average force
is ½ k x, distance moved is x, thus W = ½ k x².

### Example 16.1 — How Stiff Are Car Springs?

Force constant of a suspension that settles 1.20 cm under an 80.0 kg person:
weight w = m g = 80.0 × 9.80 = 784 N; from Hooke's law k = −F / x = −784 /
(−1.20 × 10⁻²) = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m. The car would oscillate were it not for
damping from shock absorbers.

### Example 16.2 — Calculating Stored Energy (Toy Gun Spring)

For k = 50.0 N/m, x = 0.150 m:
    PE_el = ½ k x² = ½ × 50.0 × (0.150)² = 0.563 J
A 2.00 g projectile leaves at v = √(2 PE / m) = 23.7 m/s when potential
energy converts entirely to kinetic energy.

# 16.2 Period and Frequency in Oscillations
================================================================================

When you pluck a guitar string, the resulting sound has a steady tone and lasts
a long time. Each successive vibration of the string takes the same time as the
previous one. We define periodic motion to be a motion that repeats itself at
regular time intervals, such as the guitar string or an object on a spring
moving up and down. The time to complete one oscillation remains constant and
is called the period T. Its units are usually seconds.

A concept closely related to period is the frequency of an event. For example,
if you get a paycheck twice a month, the frequency of payment is two per month
and the period between checks is half a month. Frequency f is defined to be the
number of events per unit time. For periodic motion, frequency is the number of
oscillations per unit time.

The relationship between frequency and period is:

    f = 1 / T          equivalently      T = 1 / f

The SI unit for frequency is the cycle per second, defined to be a hertz (Hz):
1 Hz = 1 cycle/s = 1/s. A cycle is one complete oscillation. A vibration can be
a single or multiple event, whereas oscillations are usually repetitive for a
significant number of cycles.

### Example 16.3 — Determine the Frequency of Two Oscillations

(a) Medical ultrasound oscillates with period 0.400 µs:
    f = 1 / (0.400 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 2.50 × 10⁶ Hz — well above human hearing.

(b) Middle C at f = 264 Hz has period:
    T = 1 / f = 1 / 264 Hz = 3.79 × 10⁻³ s = 3.79 ms.

# 16.3 Simple Harmonic Motion: A Special Periodic Motion
================================================================================

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) describes oscillatory systems where the net force
follows Hooke's law. The oscillations of a system in which the net force can be
described by Hooke's law are of special importance, because they are very
common.

Without damping, a simple harmonic oscillator oscillates with equal
displacement on either side of equilibrium. The maximum displacement is called
the amplitude (X). Amplitude units depend on the oscillation type — meters for
spring-mass systems, pressure units for sound.

## Key Properties

A significant characteristic of SHM is that period and frequency are
independent of amplitude. The string of a guitar, for example, will oscillate
with the same frequency whether plucked gently or hard.

Two factors determine the period:
  1. Spring stiffness (force constant k): stiffer systems have shorter periods.
  2. Mass m: more massive systems have longer periods.

In fact, the mass m and the force constant k are the only factors that affect
the period and frequency of simple harmonic motion.

## Period and Frequency Formulas

The period of a simple harmonic oscillator is:

    T = 2π √(m / k)

The frequency is:

    f = (1 / 2π) √(k / m)

Neither T nor f depends on amplitude.

### Example — Bad Shock Absorbers

For a car with mass 900 kg and suspension force constant k = 6.53 × 10⁴ N/m:
    f = (1 / 2π) √(6.53 × 10⁴ / 900) ≈ 1.36 Hz
    T = 1 / f = 0.738 s

## Link to Waves

Simple harmonic motion creates wave patterns. If a bouncing car were
photographed over time, the headlight would trace a wavelike streak. The
displacement follows sine/cosine functions.

## Mathematical Descriptions

For any SHM where the restoring force follows Hooke's law:

Displacement:
    x(t) = X cos(2π t / T)              equivalently      x(t) = A cos(ω t)

Velocity:
    v(t) = −v_max sin(2π t / T)
where v_max = 2π X / T = X √(k / m)

Acceleration:
    a(t) = −(k X / m) cos(2π t / T)

Acceleration is directly proportional to and opposite in direction to
displacement.

## Check Your Understanding

Q1. When a banjo string quiets over time, what changes?
    A. Frequency and period remain essentially unchanged. Only amplitude
    decreases as volume decreases.

Q2. Where on a wave does maximum displacement occur?
    A. Maximum displacement corresponds to the amplitude — at the wave's peak
    or trough.
